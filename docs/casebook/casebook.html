THE LENS

Designers write rules. Players feel something.

The MDA framework (Hunicke, LeBlanc, Zubek, 2004) is the most useful single idea in design. You build left to right; the player meets it right to left.

You can only change mechanics. You can only judge by feelings. Everything else on this page sits between the two.

The genre's promise: a world that goes on without you, that you can steer, and that remembers what you did.

Every chapter below is one way of keeping that promise. The failures chapter is every way of breaking it.

THE PARTS

What every game is made of

Components are the nouns. Mechanics are the verbs that act on them. Change any one and the whole game feels different.

ComponentWhat it isA medieval sim that uses it well
ResourcesThings you spend.Age of Empires II: food, wood, gold, stone. Each one gates a different kind of power.
ActorsThings with their own goals.Crusader Kings III: every vassal wants something, and some want your throne.
SpaceWhere things happen; distance and adjacency matter.Banished: the walk from home to work to stockpile is the hidden tax on everything.
TimeHow the clock runs.Medieval Dynasty: seasons last three days, the king's tax comes every spring, and your character ages.
InformationWho knows what.King of Dragon Pass never shows odds. Your council's advice is all you get, and they disagree.
GoalsWhat you're trying to do.Crusader Kings has no win. Old World ends when your dynasty completes its ambitions.
RandomnessInput randomness comes before you choose; output randomness after.An event, then a choice, feels fair. Battle Brothers' top player complaint is the other kind: a 90% hit misses and a veteran dies.
PeopleCharacters with memory.Mount & Blade: execute a captured lord and the other lords remember.
RhythmThe beat the game repeats.Thronefall: build by day, hold the walls by night.
FeedbackHow the game tells you what happened.Kingdom Two Crowns: no numbers at all. Coins drop, walls rise, villagers cheer.
Part one

What players feel

01 · FUN

Fun is learning, and choosing

Raph Koster: a game is fun while you're still learning it, and boring once you've solved it. Sid Meier: a game is a series of interesting decisions.

02 · IMMERSIVE

The player forgets the screen

Immersion comes from a world that answers you, shows instead of tells, and never breaks its own spell.

03 · ENGAGING

One more turn, one more year, one more heir

What keeps people playing tonight: goals at every distance, loops that pull, and tension that rises and falls on purpose.

04 · WORTH PLAYING

It means something, and it's different next time

What makes people come back for a second run and still think about the game a week later.

05 · WORTH SHARING

A story they have to tell someone

Games spread through stories, clips and screenshots players make themselves. Design for the retelling.

Part two

The genre's own craft

06 · LIVING TOWNS

A town you could live in

The town is the genre's face. It feels alive when people have homes, wants and routines you can see, and when their lives leave marks on the place.

07 · ECONOMY AND LOGISTICS

Goods have to walk

In a medieval sim the economy is not a number; it's carts, backs and distance. The best ones make that movement visible, so shortages have a cause you can find.

08 · SEASONS AND SURVIVAL

The year has an exam

Winter, harvest and sickness give a sim its stakes. Pressure works when it grows out of choices you made months ago, and stops working the moment it's solved.

09 · WAR AND SIEGES

Battles that weigh something

Great medieval war is about people breaking, food running out and walls you built yourself. The fight is the end of a story that started on the march.

10 · A WORLD YOU RIDE THROUGH

You are nobody, at first

The open-world medieval sim puts you on the ground. The world has its own business; your place in it is earned, and the world remembers how.

11 · DYNASTIES AND CHARACTERS

Play the family, not the map

The genre's biggest idea since 2012: you are a person, then their child. Death becomes a turn of the page, and every marriage is a bet on the next three rulers.

12 · RULERS AND THE COURT

Rule people, not a spreadsheet

Decisions bite when they land on people with their own memory and loyalty. The court is where a strategy game becomes a drama.

Part three

Holding it together

13 · EMERGENT STORIES

Stories no one wrote, on purpose

Emergent means it comes out of the rules, not a script. Designers can't write these stories, but they can make them likely, readable and worth retelling.

14 · PACING A LONG CAMPAIGN

Hour forty should still have a question

The genre's oldest problem: the opening is tense, the middle snowballs, the end is admin. Pacing means planning the threat curve across the whole reign.

15 · ONBOARDING

A front door into a deep sim

Depth is the genre's selling point and its top complaint. The best games keep the first hour simple and add the depth behind it.

16 · CAMERA AND READABILITY

Read the realm from far, meet a person up close

A world sim lives at many zooms. Far out you read the state of things; close in you feel them. Each zoom has its own job.

17 · GAME THEORY

How players (and AI rivals) reason

Game theory is the maths of choices that depend on other people's choices. These ideas show up in every strategy game, and most of all at a medieval court.

Part four

Where it goes wrong

18 · THE GENRE'S FAILURES

How medieval sims lose their players

The same few problems show up across hundreds of thousands of reviews: the late game goes quiet, the systems hide their reasons, the chores never end, and the world stops reacting.
